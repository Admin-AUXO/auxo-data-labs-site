**In short:** AI makes one step of the work faster. The whole process still moves at the pace of its slowest step, and that step is usually a decision. Find it before you buy anything.

## The update got faster. The decision didn't.

Picture a founder who sets up an AI assistant to draft the monthly investor update. What took two days now takes an hour. It feels like a win, and it is one.

Then the board call arrives on the same day as always. The same numbers get debated. The same decisions get pushed to next month.

Nothing went wrong with the AI. It did its step well. It just wasn't the step holding things up.

<Callout>
Every process has a slowest step. Speed up any other step and the work simply waits longer in front of the slow one.
</Callout>

You can see this in real teams. Faros AI, which sells engineering analytics, studied telemetry from over 10,000 developers in a 2025 report. On teams with high AI use, developers completed 21% more tasks and merged 98% more pull requests. The time spent reviewing those pull requests rose 91%. Its reading: the gains stall at human approval ([Faros AI](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-software-engineering)).

## Look at the workflow around the task

In McKinsey's survey, out of 25 attributes tested, redesigning workflows had the biggest effect on whether organisations saw an EBIT impact from generative AI. Yet only 21% of respondents whose organisations used it said they had fundamentally redesigned at least some workflows ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai-how-organizations-are-rewiring-to-capture-value)).

So before you pick a tool, ask three questions. Which decision does this work feed? Who makes it? What is the slowest step in front of it?

Often the answer has nothing to do with technology. Move the meeting. Let a named person approve spend below a set amount. Agree in advance which number triggers action, such as runway under a set number of months, so the board call confirms a decision and moves on.

## Count days, and trust the count

Measure the days from month-end close to a decision. Time saved on one step tells you little, and how fast the work feels tells you less.

In a 2025 trial by METR, experienced developers using AI tools took 19% longer to finish tasks, yet believed afterwards that AI had sped them up by 20% ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)). METR's 2026 update says newer tools probably do speed developers up, but it still treats self-reported gains as unreliable ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)).

## Some steps should stay slow

A senior hire, a large contract or a price change deserves your full attention. More AI-drafted options can make those calls harder. And if a decision happens once a quarter, saving a day on the draft may not matter much.

## What to do next

Before you add AI to a task, find the decision it feeds and look at the slowest step in front of it. That is usually where the time is.
