"Why can't Claude, ChatGPT or an agent we already have do this?"

## In short

Often it can do part of it. The model is rarely the hard part. The hard part is the data it reads, the systems it connects to, the rules on what it may do, who approves its work, and keeping it running. If your deck, your model and your accounts disagree on ARR, an assistant will not settle it. It will pick one answer, with confidence.

## General assistants already deliver for individuals

In a study of more than 5,000 customer support agents, access to a generative AI assistant raised issues resolved per hour by 14% on average, and by 34% for novice and less skilled workers ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161)). That is a real gain on a well-scoped, repeated task.

A preliminary 2025 report from MIT's NANDA initiative found workers at 90% of the companies it studied using personal AI tools, while only 40% of those companies had bought an official subscription ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/ai/mit-report-misunderstood-shadow-ai-economy-booms-while-headlines-cry-failure)).

If the job is one person, one task, information they can paste in, and a person reading the result before it goes anywhere, an assistant is often enough. Drafting an investor update. Summarising customer calls. Buy a team plan, write down what data may go in, and get on with it.

## The trouble starts when the work belongs to the company

The task stops being one person's. Now the assistant has to read live data from your CRM, billing tool and accounts, act inside them, and be right often enough that people stop checking every line.

That is where reliability drops. Salesforce AI Research tested leading agents on realistic CRM tasks. They succeeded about 58% of the time on single-turn tasks and about 35% when the work ran over several turns. They also showed near-zero awareness of what was confidential ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18878)). Those were 2025 models, and newer ones score higher. The point is the shape: multi-step work on real data is harder than one question in a chat window.

It shows in results too. McKinsey's 2025 survey found 88% of organisations use AI somewhere, but only 39% report any impact on EBIT at the enterprise level ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/the-state-of-ai-2025)). Using AI and getting value from it are different things.

## Why pilots stall

Pilots rarely stall because the model was too weak. S&P Global found that 42% of companies abandoned most of their AI initiatives in 2025, up from 17% the year before. Cost, privacy and security were among the top obstacles ([CIO Dive](https://www.ciodive.com/news/AI-project-fail-data-SPGlobal/742590/)). Gartner predicted in 2024 that at least 30% of generative AI projects would be dropped after proof of concept by the end of 2025, citing poor data quality, weak risk controls, rising costs and unclear business value ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2024-07-29-gartner-predicts-30-percent-of-generative-ai-projects-will-be-abandoned-after-proof-of-concept-by-end-of-2025)).

The causes sit in the setup.

### The data is not ready

The pilot ran on a clean export. The real data lives in a CRM, a billing tool and a spreadsheet that disagree about what a customer, a deal or a month of revenue is. In RSM's 2025 survey of middle-market firms using generative AI, 41% named data quality as a top rollout challenge ([RSM](https://rsmus.com/newsroom/2025/middle-market-firms-rapidly-embracing-generative-ai-but-expertise-gaps-pose-risks-rsm-2025-ai-survey.html)).

### The connections are not there

A demo can copy and paste. Production needs live connections, the right permissions and somewhere for the output to land. Microsoft tells its own customers to fix oversharing, meaning loose permissions and sensitive files anyone can reach, before rolling out Microsoft Copilot and agents ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/copilot/agent-essentials/agent-essentials-overview)). In a startup, that includes the shared drive behind your data room.

### Nobody owns it or approves it

The pilot was someone's side project, and it stops when they leave. Who checks the output before it reaches an investor, a customer or the ledger? OpenAI's own guide to building agents says actions that are sensitive, irreversible or high-stakes should trigger human oversight ([OpenAI](https://cdn.openai.com/business-guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-agents.pdf)).

### Running it costs more than the pilot

Anthropic's engineers report that agents use about four times the tokens of a chat, and multi-agent systems about fifteen times ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). Gartner expects at least half of generative AI projects to overrun their budgets through 2028, ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/articles/hype-cycle-for-genai)).

## The cost of doing it yourself

Doing it yourself is often the right call. The licence is the cheap part. The cost is the work around the model. Someone has to connect the systems, clean the data, write the rules, build tests, and fix things when a vendor changes an API or a model. Anthropic advises testing an agent on 20 to 50 tasks drawn from real failures, and warns that without that, problems get caught only in production ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)).

If that work goes to a new hire, budget for a full senior salary, before recruiting, benefits and tools ([Built In](https://builtin.com/salaries/us/data-engineer)). If it goes to a founder or your first engineer in spare evenings, the cost is hidden but real. It comes out of the job you hired them for, usually in the weeks before a raise.

In the same RSM survey, 92% hit problems rolling out generative AI, and 70% of those using it said they need outside help to get full value from it ([RSM](https://rsmus.com/newsroom/2025/middle-market-firms-rapidly-embracing-generative-ai-but-expertise-gaps-pose-risks-rsm-2025-ai-survey.html)).

Anthropic's own advice is to find the simplest solution that works and add complexity only when needed ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-effective-agents)). We agree.

## What to ask before you buy another AI tool

1. **What exact task will it do, and who does that task today?**
2. **Which data does it need?** Is it correct, and in one place?
3. **Which systems must it read from or write to?** Who grants access?
4. **What may it do without a person?** Name the approver for the rest.
5. **How will you know it works?** Test it on 20 real examples before launch.
6. **What will it cost at real volume**, not pilot volume?
7. **Who owns it after launch?** What if they leave?
8. **Can you export your data and leave?** Is the account in your company's name?

If you cannot answer the first three, the tool is not your next purchase. The data and the process are.

## When not to hire anyone

There are times you should not hire us or anyone else.

- **Your team uses Claude, ChatGPT or Microsoft Copilot well.** People check the output, and the work is drafting and analysis. Keep going.
- **An agent you already run works.** It has an owner, someone checks it, and the cost is known. Replacing a working tool is cost with no gain.
- **There is no repeated process yet.** If the task happens twice a quarter, automation will not pay back.
- **The stakes are low and the action is easy to undo.**
