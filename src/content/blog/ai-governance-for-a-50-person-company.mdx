**In short:** AI governance for a company of 50 is four named roles and four questions: who decides, who approves, who can see what and what gets logged. No committee is needed. The written rule is a separate job, covered in our [one-page AI policy template](/blog/a-one-page-ai-policy-for-a-company-of-50/).

Someone on your team drafts investor updates with AI. Someone else has connected it to the CRM.

Nobody decided who approves what it produces or what it can see. You have no compliance team, and most guides assume you do.

## Four questions for every piece of AI work

- **Who decides.** One person says whether the AI does this job at all, and stops it if it goes wrong.
- **Who approves.** A named person reads what the AI drafts, sorts or flags before it goes out, gets paid or changes a record.
- **Who can see what.** Each tool reads only the data its job needs. Read access is the default. Write access is the exception.
- **What gets logged.** Who asked, what ran, on which data, who approved it and when.

Behind these sits a fifth: what does the data mean? See [agree what the numbers mean before AI reads them](/blog/agree-what-the-numbers-mean-before-ai-reads-them/).

## Four roles, each with a name

One person can hold more than one role. Each needs a name beside it.

**The accountable owner.** One person answers for how the company uses AI and decides what it may and may not do. At seed to Series B this is usually a founder or the COO.

**The approver, one per AI output.** The investor update goes to the CFO or finance lead. The customer reply goes to the support lead. See [name the approver before you build the agent](/blog/name-the-approver-before-you-build-the-agent/).

**The access owner.** One person controls which tools connect to which systems. At this size that is often the head of engineering, an ops lead with admin rights or an outside IT provider. They review access every quarter and remove it when someone leaves.

**The data owner.** One person per important source: the CRM, billing, the finance system, the shared drive. They decide who may read it and what its fields mean.

If any role is empty, you have found your first job.

## What the big frameworks add

The [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) was released in January 2023. NIST describes it as intended for voluntary use. It sets out four functions: Govern, Map, Measure and Manage.

[ISO/IEC 42001](https://www.iso.org/standard/42001) is an international standard for an AI management system. ISO describes it as setting out requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining and continually improving one. Companies can choose to be audited against it. That is a larger project than most 50-person startups need today.

Treat both as reading. This post does not meet either one for you. If an investor or customer later asks for one, you will already have the roles and logs to build on.

## The first month

**1. List every AI tool in use.** Include personal accounts. One line per tool: what it does, what data it touches, who uses it.

**2. Fill the four roles.** Put a name beside the owner, each approver, the access owner and each data owner. Share the list with the company.

**3. Pick one workflow and add the controls.** Choose the one where a mistake costs most. Set the tool to read-only. Put the approval step in the workflow itself, not in a document. Turn on logging.

After a month, check the setup again. [Will it still run when the builder leaves?](/blog/will-it-still-run-when-the-builder-leaves/) has five checks.

## What to leave out for now

Leave out the 40-page policy, the ethics board and certification. Four named people do more than a committee that meets twice a year. Keep the roles, the approvals, the access list and the log.
