**In short:** an AI readiness assessment checks whether the data an AI tool would read is accurate, complete, consistent, timely and trusted, and who owns it and approves its use. It comes before you buy the tool.

You want to use AI on real work. Someone says you should run an AI readiness assessment first. Nobody explains what that means.

Here is what it looks at, what it leaves out, and how to run a quick version yourself.

## What the check looks at

An AI readiness assessment asks one question: can an AI tool read your data and get the right answer? We use five checks.

- **Accurate.** The number matches reality. The ARR in your CRM matches the invoices in your accounts.
- **Complete.** The fields you rely on are filled in. If a tool must sort leads by country, most leads need a country.
- **Consistent.** One word means one thing. "Active customer" is the same in sales, finance and operations. See [agree what the numbers mean before AI reads them](/blog/agree-what-the-numbers-mean-before-ai-reads-them/).
- **Timely.** The data is fresh enough for the job. A pipeline report that updates weekly cannot answer a question about today.
- **Trusted.** People use it without a side spreadsheet. If your finance lead rebuilds the numbers by hand every month, the system is not trusted yet.

The same lens covers who owns each source, what the tool may touch and who approves its work.

## What it is not

- **Not a security review.** It notes who can see what. It does not test your defences.
- **Not a compliance certificate.** It does not tell you that you meet a standard. For risk management, the voluntary [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) is a useful reference.
- **Not a purchase decision made for you.** A good check can end with "you do not need this tool yet".

## Why it matters

Gartner reports that [63% of organisations](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-02-26-lack-of-ai-ready-data-puts-ai-projects-at-risk) either lack the right data management practices for AI or are unsure they have them. It also predicts that organisations will abandon 60% of AI projects not supported by AI-ready data.

The model is rarely the weak point. See [the model is the easy part](/blog/the-model-is-the-easy-part/).

## Run a quick one in an afternoon

Pick one use case, not the whole company. For example: an assistant that sorts inbound leads, or one that drafts the monthly investor update. Then answer these.

1. Which systems hold the data this task reads?
2. Who owns each one, by name?
3. Pull 20 real records. Are the key fields filled in?
4. Check five of them against a second source. Do they match?
5. Do two teams define the main terms differently?
6. How old is the data when the tool reads it? Is that fast enough?
7. Does anyone keep a private copy because they do not trust the system?
8. What may the tool read, and what may it change?
9. Who approves its work before anything goes out?
10. If it gets one wrong, can you see what it read and why?

Write each answer down in one line.

## What good and bad answers look like

A good answer is specific: "The CRM, owned by the head of sales. 19 of 20 records have a country." A bad answer is vague or missing: "Probably the shared drive." "Nobody owns it." That is not a failure. It is the finding. Watch for "it depends who you ask". That is a consistency problem, and the most common one.

## What to fix first

1. **Name an owner** for each data source the use case needs. Nothing else sticks without this.
2. **Write down the five to ten terms** the task depends on, with one sentence each.
3. **Fill the gaps** in the few fields the task reads. Ignore the rest.
4. **Set the approval step.** A person approves anything sent, posted, paid or decided.
5. **Only then** choose the tool.
