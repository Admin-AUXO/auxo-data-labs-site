**In short:** "A person approves what goes out" only works if you decide which person, for which actions, seeing what. Write that down for every action before the agent exists.

## Who answers for what your AI says?

You do. In 2024, a Canadian tribunal ordered Air Canada to compensate a customer after its website chatbot gave him wrong information about bereavement fares. The airline argued the chatbot was a separate legal entity. The tribunal found the airline responsible for all the information on its website, chatbot included ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2024-february/bc-tribunal-confirms-companies-remain-liable-information-provided-ai-chatbot/)). It was a small-claims ruling, not binding precedent.

Now picture the startup version: an agent that emails a customer, quotes a price or sends a number to an investor.

In a McKinsey survey published in March 2025, 27% of respondents whose organisations use generative AI said employees review all of its output before use. A similar share said 20% or less gets checked ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai-how-organizations-are-rewiring-to-capture-value)).

## How much approval does each action need?

Match it to the risk. OpenAI's guide to building agents suggests rating each tool low, medium or high, based on read or write access, whether the action can be reversed, the permissions it needs and its financial impact. It says sensitive, irreversible or high-stakes actions, such as large refunds or payments, should go to a person ([OpenAI](https://cdn.openai.com/business-guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-agents.pdf)).

For each line, write down the approver, what they see and how fast they must respond. If nobody responds, nothing goes out.

## How do you stop approval becoming a rubber stamp?

Anyone facing hundreds of drafts a day will start clicking yes. That is automation bias, and it says nothing about the person.

A systematic review of research on clinical decision support, published in the Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association, found that heavy workload and time pressure make people more likely to over-rely on automated advice, and that making users accountable helps ([JAMIA](https://academic.oup.com/jamia/article/19/1/121/732254)). The EU AI Act requires high-risk AI systems to be built so the people overseeing them can stay aware of this tendency, and can override or disregard the output ([EU AI Act, Article 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)).

So design for real review. Show the source next to the draft. Keep the queue small enough to read, or narrow the agent's job. Track how often approvers edit or reject. A long run with no edits may mean nobody is reading.

## Where can you skip approval?

Internal summaries and easy-to-undo actions. Save attention for mistakes that would reach a customer, an investor or the ledger.

## What to do next

For each action the agent can take, write who approves it, what they see, how fast they respond and what happens if they don't.
