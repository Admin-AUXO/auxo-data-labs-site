"We already pay for Claude. Our engineers have built an agent or two. Why would we need anyone else?"

## In short

Often you don't. Claude or an agent you built can do part of the job. The model is rarely the hard part. The hard part is the data it reads, the systems it connects to, the rules on what it may do, who approves its work, and keeping it running as things change. This is the technical version, for the engineer or CTO who would build it and keep it running.

## Start with what already works

Claude, ChatGPT and Microsoft Copilot help people draft, summarise, write code and analyse. We often recommend them ourselves.

In a study of more than 5,000 customer support agents, access to a generative AI assistant raised issues resolved per hour [by 14% on average](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161). Narrow steps go well too: Salesforce AI Research's CRMArena-Pro benchmark found leading agents scored above 83% on single-turn workflow execution ([arXiv, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18878)).

If your team uses an assistant on well-scoped tasks, keep doing it. The trouble starts when the work becomes a company process.

## The system around the model is most of the work

A production agent is a model plus everything around it. Take an agent that answers board questions about ARR, churn and runway. It reads from your CRM, billing tool and accounts. Then it needs:

- **Data contracts.** If "customer", "active" or "ARR" means three things in three systems, the agent picks one and you won't know which. Agree the definitions and check them at the source.
- **Tool permissions.** Every tool is a permission. Read access to a pipeline report is a different risk from write access to your billing system.
- **Evaluation sets.** Real tasks with known good answers, run on every change. Without them, "it seems better" is your only measure.
- **Observability.** A trace of every run: inputs, context, tool calls, outputs and cost. When a number is wrong, you need to replay it.
- **Cost and latency budgets.** Agents loop. A cheap demo gets expensive at volume.
- **Human approval and audit logs.** Decide which outputs a person approves, and build that step into the workflow. Log who asked, what ran, on which data, and who signed off. Investors and auditors will ask in diligence.
- **Versioning.** Models, prompts and data change. Pin versions and re-run your evaluations before you switch.
- **Security and data residency.** Where the data goes, who sees it, whether the vendor keeps it, and which region it sits in. In the UAE, the US and Europe, that answer can carry legal weight.

None of this depends on how capable the model is. A better model does not fix a missing data contract.

## Where it breaks in practice

These benchmarks tested 2024 and 2025 models, and newer ones score higher. The pattern still shows where the work gets hard.

TheAgentCompany is a research benchmark of 175 realistic office tasks in a simulated company ([arXiv, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14161)). The best agent tested fully completed about 30% of them. The hardest tasks involved talking to colleagues and working through complex office web apps ([The Register, June 2025](https://www.theregister.com/2025/06/29/ai_agents_fail_a_lot/)).

CRMArena-Pro tested agents on realistic CRM data. Success fell from about 58% on single-turn tasks to about 35% when the work ran over several turns. The authors also found the agents showed "near-zero inherent confidentiality awareness" ([arXiv, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18878)). A model will not protect data it was never told is sensitive. Permissions and filtering have to sit outside it.

Cost behaves the same way. Anthropic's own engineers report that agents use about four times the tokens of a chat, and multi-agent systems about 15 times ([Anthropic, June 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). They also found multi-agent setups did much better on research tasks, so the cost can be worth paying for valuable work.

And projects stall. In S&P Global's 2025 survey, 42% of companies said they had abandoned most of their AI initiatives, up from 17% the year before. Cost, data privacy and security were among the top obstacles ([CIO Dive, March 2025](https://www.ciodive.com/news/AI-project-fail-data-SPGlobal/742590/)). None of those is a model problem.

## The model makers say the same

Anthropic's guide to [building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-effective-agents) recommends starting with the simplest setup that works and adding agents only when needed. Its guide to [evaluating agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents) says teams without evaluations end up catching problems in production, and suggests starting with 20 to 50 tasks drawn from real failures.

OpenAI's [practical guide to building agents](https://cdn.openai.com/business-guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-agents.pdf) tells teams to rate each tool by risk and send sensitive, irreversible or high-stakes actions to a person.

Microsoft's [deployment blueprint](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/copilot/secure-govern-copilot-foundational-deployment-guidance) asks customers to fix oversharing before rolling out Copilot and agents. Its [2025 Responsible AI Transparency Report](https://cdn-dynmedia-1.microsoft.com/is/content/microsoftcorp/microsoft/msc/documents/presentations/CSR/Responsible-AI-Transparency-Report-2025-vertical.pdf) says enterprise customers must put their own human oversight and access controls in place.

Three model makers, one message. They supply the model. Data readiness, evaluation and oversight are the customer's job.

## A checklist before an agent touches real work

Ten clear yeses.

1. A named owner for the agent, and one for each data source it reads.
2. Written definitions for the fields it relies on, checked at the source.
3. An evaluation set of real tasks, run before every model, prompt or data change.
4. Least-privilege access: read by default, write only where needed, nothing irreversible without approval.
5. A human approval step for anything sent, posted, paid or decided.
6. A trace and audit log of every run: who asked, what ran, on which data, who approved it.
7. A cost ceiling and a latency target, with alerts on both.
8. Pinned model and prompt versions, with a tested path to upgrade.
9. A clear answer on where data is processed and stored, and whether the vendor keeps it.
10. Code, documentation, accounts and access in your company's name, so it runs without the people who built it.

If your current agent has all ten, keep it. Swapping models or vendors for its own sake adds risk and little else. If it has three, the model was never the problem.
