**In short:** a company AI policy fits on one page. It says which tools are approved, what data may go in, who approves AI output, who owns the result and what to do when something goes wrong.

Your people already use AI tools. Some use a work account. Some use a personal one.

Nobody wrote the rule down. So nobody knows what is allowed, and nobody is checking.

This post gives you an AI policy template that fits on one page, for a company of around 50 people.

## Why a one-page rule beats a long one

A long AI governance policy gets signed and never read. A one-page company AI policy gets used, because people can hold it in their head.

At 50 people, you do not need a committee. You need five answers, written plainly, with a name next to each. If the rule is short, the owner can update it when a tool changes.

Short also means honest. A policy that bans everything gets ignored, and people use their phones instead. A policy that allows what is safe gives them an approved way to work.

If you want a longer reference later, read the [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework). NIST describes it as intended for voluntary use. Or read the summary of [ISO/IEC 42001](https://www.iso.org/standard/42001), a standard for running an AI management system. Treat both as reading. Neither is a thing you need on day one.

## What the policy must answer

An AI acceptable use policy comes down to five questions.

1. **What data may go into an AI tool?** Name the data that never goes in: customer personal data, contracts under NDA, payroll, credentials, unreleased financials. Name what is fine: public information and your own drafts.
2. **Which tools are approved?** List them by name, and say which account type. Vendors treat plan types differently. OpenAI, for example, [says it does not train on business data by default](https://openai.com/enterprise-privacy/), while consumer plans work differently. Read the data page of each tool you approve.
3. **Who approves AI output before it leaves the company?** AI drafts, sorts, checks and flags. A person approves anything sent to a customer, a supplier, a candidate or a regulator. We cover how to design that step in [name the approver before you build the agent](/blog/name-the-approver-before-you-build-the-agent/).
4. **Who owns the data and the outputs?** Company data stays company data. Work made with AI in the course of the job belongs to the company, subject to your contracts and local law. Name one owner for each tool and each data source.
5. **What happens when something goes wrong?** Say who to tell, how fast, and that nobody is punished for reporting in good faith.

## A template you can copy

Paste this into a document. Replace every bracket. Keep it to one page.

> **[Company] AI policy**
>
> **1. Purpose.** This policy sets the rules for using AI tools at [Company]. It applies to every employee, contractor and intern.
>
> **2. Approved tools.** Use only these tools, on company accounts: [Tool A], [Tool B], [Tool C]. [Name] keeps this list. To request a new tool, ask [Name].
>
> **3. Data that never goes into an AI tool.** Customer personal data. Information covered by an NDA. Payroll and HR records. Passwords, keys and access tokens. Unreleased financial results. [Add your own.]
>
> **4. Data that may go in.** Public information. Your own drafts. Internal material marked [Level], in approved tools only.
>
> **5. Approval before release.** AI may draft, summarise, sort and check. A person reads and approves anything that leaves the company. [Name] approves customer and supplier messages. [Name] approves anything involving money, contracts or hiring. If the approver is unavailable, the item waits.
>
> **6. Ownership.** Company data and the work you produce for [Company] stay with [Company]. [Name] owns each approved tool and its access list.
>
> **7. When something goes wrong.** If sensitive data went into the wrong tool, or AI output went out unchecked, tell [Name] the same day. Reporting early is the right thing to do. No blame for good-faith reports.
>
> **8. Review.** [Name] reviews this policy every [quarter] and after any incident.

## Roll it out in a week

You can finish this in five working days.

- **Day 1.** Ask each team lead which AI tools their people use. Include personal accounts. You want the real list.
- **Day 2.** Pick the approved tools. Check each vendor's data page. Choose the owner for each.
- **Day 3.** Fill in the template. One person writes it. Two people read it.
- **Day 4.** Share it in a 20-minute team call. Show two examples: one that is fine, one that is not.
- **Day 5.** Turn on the controls you can: business accounts, sign-in through your company login, and a single place to report problems.

Then put a review date in the calendar. A policy with no review date is out of date in six months. ## When to ask a lawyer

This post is not legal advice. It is a plain starting point for an internal rule.

Ask your own legal adviser before you finalise the policy if you handle health, financial or children's data, if you work under sector rules, if your contracts limit how client data is used, or if you sell into the EU. Rules differ between the UAE, the US and Europe. Legal checks stay with your advisers. AUXO does not give legal advice and does not certify anyone.

The policy is a business rule. A lawyer tells you whether it is enough for your situation.
