**In short:** Much useful AI work is repetitive: sorting, tagging and pulling fields out of text. A small, fast model such as Claude Haiku 5.5 often fits this better than a large one. It is quicker and costs a fraction as much.

## Does every task need the biggest model?

Picture a support lead on a Monday morning, with a weekend of tickets waiting to be sorted. Think of the jobs your team does over and over. Tagging a ticket. Routing a lead. Reading an expense line and picking the ledger account. Each one is small. Together they add up to thousands a month.

For work like this, the question is less "how clever is the model?" and more "how fast and how cheap is it, per item?"

## Why smaller can be better

Anthropic positions Haiku 5.5 for high-volume, time-sensitive tasks such as classification, extraction and routing. It is the fastest of the current Claude models and costs a small fraction of the larger ones.

That gap matters at volume. A task that runs ten times a month can use any model. A task that runs fifty thousand times a month shows up in your costs, and in how long people wait.

Speed matters too. If an inbound lead is routed in under a second, it reaches the right person while it is still warm. If a support ticket is tagged as it arrives, the urgent ones rise to the top straight away.

Anthropic's [guidance on choosing a model](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/models/choosing-a-model) describes Haiku as suited to cost-sensitive work that still needs strong reasoning. It supports the same [effort](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/effort) levels as the larger models, so you can ask it to think harder where needed.

## Good high-volume tasks

**Finance.** Coding expense lines to the right ledger account. Matching payments to open invoices. Flagging invoices where the total does not match the line items, for a person to check.

**Sales.** Routing inbound leads by segment, region and urgency. Tagging CRM call notes with the topics raised. Spotting duplicate contacts in a CRM export.

**Support.** Tagging tickets by product and urgency. Sorting feedback into themes. Flagging tickets that mention cancelling.

**Engineering.** Labelling bug reports by component. Pulling error codes and versions out of logs and issue threads.

**Content.** Tagging an archive of articles by topic. Writing short alt text for product images.

There is a right answer, and a person can spot a wrong one quickly.

## What makes it work

Small models do well when the task is tightly defined.

- **Fixed categories.** Give the model a closed list of labels, with a one-line definition for each. "Urgent" means different things to different teams. Write down what it means to yours.
- **Examples.** Include a handful of real, correctly labelled examples, including awkward ones.
- **Structured output.** Ask for the same fields in the same format every time, so results drop straight into your sheet or system.
- **An "unsure" option.** Let the model say it is not sure. Send those cases to a person rather than forcing a guess.
- **Spot checks.** A person reviews a sample each week. Volume hides errors unless someone looks.

## When to move up

Some work needs judgement rather than sorting. Writing a reply, weighing a trade-off or explaining why a number moved is a different kind of job. Move up to Sonnet or Opus for that.

A common pattern is to combine the two. A fast model sorts and extracts at volume. A larger model handles the small share of cases that are genuinely hard. Anthropic describes this approach in its own guidance on balancing cost and intelligence.

## Try this

Pick one sorting or extraction task your team does by hand. Export the last 100 examples, with the answer a person gave.

1. Write a one-page instruction: the categories or fields, a definition for each, and five worked examples.
2. Run the 100 examples through Haiku 5.5 at its default effort.
3. Compare its answers with your team's. Count the matches, and read every mismatch.

Often the mismatches show that your own team disagrees on the categories. Fix the definitions, then run it again.
