**In short:** Most data and AI projects are scoped as a build, and the real test comes when the person who built it moves on. In a startup, that is often the one engineer or contractor who knows why the board deck says what it says. Five checks tell you whether you own the system or depend on one person.

## Who else can fix it?

Picture a founder a week before a raise. The metrics pipeline has run for a year, and the engineer who built it has just handed in notice.

Nobody else has opened the code. The board deck still needs its numbers on the 1st.

That moment is avoidable, and the checks below are how.

## Plan for the builder to leave

They will. In the US, the median time workers had been with their current employer was 4.1 years in January 2026, and 3.0 years for workers aged 25 to 34 ([US Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/tenure.nr0.htm)). If a pipeline, a report or an AI agent must run for years, plan for it to outlive its builder.

## Systems decay, then diligence finds out

They rarely break on day one. A formula only one person understood stops matching the business after a pricing change, and ARR drifts from the accounts. An API key expires, and it lived in a former contractor's personal account. An agent's rules were tuned by one engineer, and nobody else knows why it skips certain customers. The model it runs on is retired by the vendor. Anthropic, for example, publishes a schedule for this, and once a model is retired, requests to it fail ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/model-deprecations)).

Each is small. Together they leave a system people quietly work around. Then diligence arrives, and nobody can explain the numbers in the data room.

This is common in well-run teams. In Stack Overflow's 2024 developer survey, 30% of developers said knowledge silos affect their productivity ten or more times a week ([Stack Overflow](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2024/professional-developers)).

Handover is the last item in the plan and the first to be cut. The project is judged at go-live, not at month six.

## Five checks before you call it done

1. **Everything is in the company's name.** Repositories, cloud accounts, vendor contracts, API keys and admin access. Not a founder's personal email. Not a contractor's account.
2. **A written runbook exists.** What it does, why it was built that way, what to check when a number looks wrong, and who to ask.
3. **Someone else has run it.** A second person has done the monthly run, or fixed a failure, without the builder in the room.
4. **It tells you when it breaks.** Data checks and alerts flag a missing feed or an odd number before the board deck does.
5. **It has an owner.** A role, not one person's goodwill.

For AI agents, add one more. Keep the prompts, rules, approval lists and model versions written down next to the code.

## When this is overkill

A one-off analysis for a board question, a prototype you will throw away, a spreadsheet one person uses alone: leave them light, and label them temporary so nobody builds on them.

## A simple test

Ask the builder to take two weeks off. If the system keeps running and someone else can fix it, you own it. If not, you own a dependency on one person. Fix that before you build anything new on top.
